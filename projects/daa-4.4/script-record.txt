10  Three SAT. A formula in conjunctive normal form: an AND of clauses, where every clause is an OR of exactly three literals. Is it satisfiable?
11  Step one. Three SAT is in NP: the certificate is an assignment, and checking every clause takes linear time.
12  Step two. SAT reduces to three SAT. Translate every clause into clauses of exactly three literals. A long clause is split with a fresh variable: a or b or c or d becomes, a or b or y, and, not y or c or d.
13  A short clause is padded: a or b becomes, a or b or y, and, a or b or not y. The new formula is satisfiable exactly when the old one is, and the translation is linear in the size of the formula. Three SAT is NP complete.
14  Clique. Given a graph and a number k, is there a set of k vertices that are all pairwise connected? Five people who all follow each other: a clique of size five.
15  Step one. Clique is in NP: the certificate is the k vertices; check the k choose two edges between them, polynomial.
16  Step two. Three SAT reduces to clique. Take a three SAT formula with k clauses. Make one vertex for every literal of every clause: three k vertices, in k groups.
17  Draw an edge between two vertices when they are in different clauses and they are not opposites: never x with not x. Then ask for a clique of size k.
18  Our example has three clauses, so nine vertices and k equals three. Picking x three from clause one, x three from clause two and x one from clause three gives a triangle: a clique of size three.
19  Why it works. A clique of size k picks one literal from every clause with no contradictions, so setting those literals true satisfies the formula. And a satisfying assignment picks one true literal per clause, and those k vertices form a clique. Yes matches yes.
20  The graph has three k vertices and fewer than nine k squared edges: polynomial. Clique is NP complete.
21  Vertex cover. Given a graph and k, is there a set of k vertices that touches every edge? The guards at junctions from the last video.
22  Step one. Vertex cover is in NP: the certificate is the k vertices; check that every edge touches one of them, polynomial.
23  Step two. Clique reduces to vertex cover. Take the graph G and build its complement, G bar: same vertices, with an edge exactly where G has none. Ask for a cover of size n minus k.
24  Our example: five vertices, and one, two, three, four form a clique of size four. In the complement, the only edges are the three missing from G, and vertex five alone covers them. Clique of four, cover of one: one is five minus four.
25  Why it works. The k clique vertices have no complement edges among them, so every complement edge touches the other n minus k vertices: they are a cover. And the vertices outside a cover have no edges in G bar, so they are a clique in G.
26  The complement has the same n vertices and fewer than n squared edges: polynomial. Vertex cover is NP complete.
27  Three problems, one triangle. A clique in G, an independent set in G bar, and the complement of a vertex cover in G bar are the same set of vertices. Any one of them solves the others.
28  And the chain: SAT to three SAT to clique to vertex cover. Each arrow is one reduction, and hardness flows down the arrows from Cook's theorem to everything.
