10  {explain} Three SAT. A formula in conjunctive normal form: an AND of clauses, where every clause is an OR of exactly three literals. | Is it satisfiable?
11  {explain} Step one. Three SAT is in NP: | the certificate is an assignment, and checking every clause takes linear time.
12  {explain} Step two. SAT reduces to three SAT. Translate every clause into clauses of exactly three literals. | A long clause is split with a fresh variable: a or b or c or d becomes, a or b or y, and, not y or c or d.
13  {explain} A short clause is padded: a or b becomes, a or b or y, and, a or b or not y. | The new formula is satisfiable exactly when the old one is, and the translation is linear in the size of the formula. Three SAT is NP complete.
14  {explain} Clique. Given a graph and a number k, is there a set of k vertices that are all pairwise connected? | Five people who all follow each other: a clique of size five.
15  {explain} Step one. Clique is in NP: | the certificate is the k vertices; check the k choose two edges between them, polynomial.
16  {explain} Step two. Three SAT reduces to clique. Take a three SAT formula with k clauses. | Make one vertex for every literal of every clause: three k vertices, in k groups.
17  {explain} Draw an edge between two vertices when they are in different clauses and they are not opposites: never x with not x. | Then ask for a clique of size k.
18  {explain} Our example has three clauses, so nine vertices and k equals three. | Picking x three from clause one, x three from clause two and x one from clause three gives a triangle: a clique of size three.
19  {explain} Why it works. A clique of size k picks one literal from every clause with no contradictions, so setting those literals true satisfies the formula. | And a satisfying assignment picks one true literal per clause, and those k vertices form a clique. Yes matches yes.
20  {excited} The graph has three k vertices and fewer than nine k squared edges: polynomial. | Clique is NP complete.
21  {explain} Vertex cover. Given a graph and k, is there a set of k vertices that touches every edge? | The guards at junctions from the last video.
22  {explain} Step one. Vertex cover is in NP: | the certificate is the k vertices; check that every edge touches one of them, polynomial.
23  {explain} Step two. Clique reduces to vertex cover. Take the graph G and build its complement, G bar: same vertices, with an edge exactly where G has none. | Ask for a cover of size n minus k.
24  {explain} Our example: five vertices, and one, two, three, four form a clique of size four. | In the complement, the only edges are the three missing from G, and vertex five alone covers them. Clique of four, cover of one: one is five minus four.
25  {explain} Why it works. The k clique vertices have no complement edges among them, so every complement edge touches the other n minus k vertices: they are a cover. | And the vertices outside a cover have no edges in G bar, so they are a clique in G.
26  {excited} The complement has the same n vertices and fewer than n squared edges: polynomial. | Vertex cover is NP complete.
27  {explain} Three problems, one triangle. | A clique in G, an independent set in G bar, and the complement of a vertex cover in G bar are the same set of vertices. Any one of them solves the others.
28  {explain} And the chain: SAT to three SAT to clique to vertex cover. | Each arrow is one reduction, and hardness flows down the arrows from Cook's theorem to everything.
