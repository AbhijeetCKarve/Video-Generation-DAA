07  {explain} An approximation algorithm runs in polynomial time and returns a solution that is close to optimal, with a guarantee. | For a minimisation problem the ratio is the cost of our answer divided by the optimal cost. A two approximation never costs more than twice the optimum.
08  {emphatic} Why accept this? Because the alternative is exact and exponential. | Twice optimal in one second beats optimal never. And in practice the answers are usually far closer than the guarantee.
09  {explain} Three terms. The ratio rho, always at least one. A rho approximation algorithm. | And for some problems a PTAS, a polynomial time approximation scheme: within one plus epsilon of optimal for any epsilon you choose, with a running time that grows as epsilon shrinks.
10  {explain} Vertex cover again: the guards at junctions, the smallest set of vertices that touches every edge. | NP hard to solve exactly, as we proved last time.
11  {explain} The approximation. While edges remain: pick any edge u v, put both u and v into the cover, | and delete every edge that touches u or v. Repeat.
12  {explain} On this graph. Pick edge two three: the cover gets two and three, and their four edges go. Pick edge five six: five and six join, three more edges go. | Pick the last edge, four seven: four and seven join. No edges left. Cover of size six.
13  {emphatic} The proof is two lines. The edges we picked share no vertex, so they form a matching of size k, and any cover must contain an endpoint of each one: the optimum is at least k. | We used exactly two k vertices. So our cover is at most twice the optimum.
14  {explain} Here we picked three edges and used six vertices, while the optimum, two, four, five, has three. | The bound is tight. And the algorithm is polynomial: order V plus E.
15  {explain} Now TSP with the triangle inequality: going directly is never longer than going through a third city. | True for road distances, flight distances, any real map.
16  {explain} The algorithm. Build a minimum spanning tree with Prim or Kruskal. Walk around the tree, visiting every vertex, | and skip any vertex you have already seen. That walk is your tour.
17  {explain} Five cities. The spanning tree costs about eleven point seven. Walking around it costs twice that. | Shortcutting the repeats gives the tour A, D, E, C, B, back to A, about fifteen point seven. Here the shortcuts happen to reach the optimum.
18  {emphatic} Why at most twice? Remove one edge from the optimal tour and you get a spanning tree, so the minimum spanning tree costs at most the optimal tour. | The full walk costs twice the tree, at most twice optimal, and shortcuts never increase the cost. A two approximation.
19  {explain} Christofides improves this to one and a half. And without the triangle inequality, no constant ratio is possible unless P equals NP. | Hardness limits even how well you can approximate.
20  {explain} Applications. AI first. Training a neural network to the global optimum, clustering with k means, finding the smallest decision tree: all NP hard. | Gradient descent and Lloyd's algorithm are heuristics: good solutions, not optimal ones, and that is enough.
21  {explain} Planning and scheduling in AI are reductions to SAT, and modern SAT solvers handle millions of clauses. | Hard in theory, routinely solved in practice. The theory tells you where to expect trouble.
22  {explain} Big data. Exact answers on a billion rows are too slow, so we approximate on purpose. | Count distinct users with HyperLogLog in a few kilobytes, find frequent items with a Count Min Sketch, and seed clustering with k means plus plus, an order log k approximation.
23  {explain} Scheduling jobs on a cluster and placing data across machines are bin packing and partitioning problems, NP hard. | Greedy approximations like first fit decreasing stay within a constant factor of optimal, and they run at scale.
24  {emphatic} Cryptography turns hardness upside down. Everything so far wanted problems to be easy. Cryptography wants them hard. | RSA relies on factoring large numbers being hard, elliptic curve cryptography on the discrete logarithm.
25  {explain} And the newest schemes, post quantum cryptography, are built on lattice problems like the shortest vector problem, NP hard in their exact form, | which even quantum computers are not known to break. The hardness you studied this unit is the lock on your bank account.
26  {warm} So the same list of hard problems means three things. | In AI, approximate them. In big data, approximate them on purpose, for speed. In cryptography, rely on them staying hard.
