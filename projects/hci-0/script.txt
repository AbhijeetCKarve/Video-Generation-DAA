07  {explain} The course at a glance. ETCS334, Human Computer Interface, in semester eight of B Tech Computer Science, pattern twenty twenty four. | It is a programme elective, elective number six.
08  {explain} The teaching scheme: one lecture hour and two laboratory hours every week, for two credits. | So two thirds of your time in this course is in the lab, doing.
09  {emphatic} Assessment is out of fifty marks. Twenty five come from continuous assessment for theory, | and twenty five from an end oral exam based on your laboratory assignments and mini project. No term end written paper, no term end practical.
10  {explain} Prerequisites: fundamentals of programming, web technologies, HTML, CSS and JavaScript, | and software engineering. You already have all three.
11  {explain} Four course objectives: understand the fundamentals and human factors of HCI, | learn interaction design, UI patterns and prototyping, apply usability engineering and user research, and evaluate interfaces with usability testing, metrics and accessibility standards, including AI based interfaces.
12  {explain} They map to four course outcomes. CO one: explain HCI concepts, human factors and Norman's principles. CO two: apply design principles and prototyping. | CO three: analyse user needs and evaluate with heuristics and ISO nine two four one. CO four: evaluate usability and accessibility with testing, metrics and WCAG, including AI and multimodal interfaces.
13  {explain} Unit one, four hours: foundations of HCI and human factors. | What HCI is, and how interaction evolved: command line, graphical interfaces, touch, voice, and now AI based interfaces.
14  {explain} Then the human as an information processor: perception, memory and mental models, | and human error: slips, where you know what to do but your hand does something else, and mistakes, where the plan itself is wrong.
15  {emphatic} Norman's six design principles: visibility, feedback, constraints, mapping, affordances and signifiers. | The gulfs of execution and evaluation, Fitts' law for pointing, and Hick's law for choosing.
16  {warm} The case study: good and bad interaction design in everyday products and public kiosks, | like an ATM or a ticket vending machine. You will never look at one the same way again.
17  {explain} Unit two, four hours: interaction design and prototyping. | User centred design and design thinking in five steps: empathise, define, ideate, prototype, test.
18  {explain} Shneiderman's eight golden rules and UI design patterns. Visual design: layout, Gestalt principles, colour and typography. | Information architecture: navigation design and card sorting.
19  {explain} Design systems and platform guidelines, Material Design and Apple's Human Interface Guidelines, | then wireframing, and low fidelity and high fidelity prototyping.
20  {warm} The case study: wireframing and a low fidelity prototype of a university student portal or mobile app. | This is where Figma enters your life.
21  {explain} Unit three, four hours: usability engineering and user research. | The usability engineering lifecycle, and the difference between usability and user experience.
22  {explain} User research methods: interviews, surveys and observation. Then making sense of the data: | affinity diagrams, personas, scenarios and user journey maps.
23  {explain} Hierarchical task analysis, and the Keystroke Level Model, which predicts how long an expert takes for a task. | Nielsen's ten usability heuristics, and the ISO nine two four one dash eleven definition of usability.
24  {warm} Heuristic evaluation with severity ratings, and the cognitive walkthrough. | The case study: a heuristic evaluation of a real app, e-commerce, banking or a government service.
25  {explain} Unit four, three hours: usability testing, accessibility and emerging interfaces. | Usability testing: the think aloud protocol, moderated and remote testing, and A B testing.
26  {explain} Usability metrics: task success rate, time on task, error rate, and the System Usability Scale, SUS. | Accessibility: WCAG two point two, the POUR principles, assistive technologies and inclusive design.
27  {emphatic} Ethics in HCI: dark patterns and privacy. | And human AI interaction: guidelines for chatbot, voice and multimodal interfaces, the newest part of the subject.
28  {warm} The case study: an accessibility evaluation of a website, or a usability evaluation of an AI chatbot. | That is the theory: fifteen hours across four units.
29  {explain} Now the laboratory, where most of your marks are made. Thirty hours, eight assignments, and real tools: | Figma or Balsamiq, Google Forms, a card sorting tool, the WAVE and Lighthouse accessibility checkers, and the NVDA screen reader.
30  {explain} Assignment one: analyse two good and two bad designs with Norman's principles. Assignment two: a survey or interview with at least five users, then personas, a journey map, task analysis and a KLM estimate. | Assignment three: card sorting with two or three users, a sitemap, and a navigation review.
31  {explain} Assignment four, the biggest at six hours: low fidelity wireframes for at least three screens, turned into an interactive high fidelity Figma prototype. | Assignment five: a heuristic evaluation against Nielsen's ten heuristics, with severity ratings and a cognitive walkthrough.
32  {explain} Assignment six: a think aloud usability test of your own prototype with three to five users, recording task success, time on task, error rate and the SUS score. | Assignment seven: an accessibility audit of a public website with WAVE, Lighthouse and NVDA, compared against WCAG two point two.
33  {excited} Assignment eight is the mini project: design, prototype and evaluate an interface through the complete user centred design process. | Suggested topics include an accessible e-governance app, a campus navigation app, a chatbot for student queries, an appointment app for elderly users, a voice interface for visually impaired users, a checkout redesign validated by A B testing, an AR campus guide, and a banking app without dark patterns.
34  {warm} Notice the flow: the assignments are the stages of one design process, | research, structure, prototype, evaluate, test, audit, and the mini project puts them together.
35  {explain} Assessment in detail. Continuous assessment for theory is twenty five marks: | CAT one, active learning, ten marks; CAT two, task based learning, ten marks; CAT three, a presentation, five marks.
36  {emphatic} The end oral exam is twenty five marks, and it is based on your laboratory assignments and the mini project. | So your lab work is not a side activity. It is half the course.
37  {explain} The textbooks: Dix, Finlay, Abowd and Beale, Human Computer Interaction; Jakob Nielsen, Usability Engineering; | and Shneiderman and colleagues, Designing the User Interface, sixth edition.
38  {explain} For reading you will enjoy: Don Norman's The Design of Everyday Things, and Steve Krug's Don't Make Me Think. | Online: the WCAG two point two guidelines, Nielsen Norman Group's ten heuristics, Microsoft's guidelines for human AI interaction, and NPTEL courses from IIT Guwahati, IIIT Hyderabad and IIT Roorkee.
39  {emphatic} Tip one. | Keep a design portfolio from week one: every survey, affinity diagram, persona, wireframe and Figma link. The oral exam is a conversation about that portfolio.
40  {emphatic} Tip two. | Start the mini project early, and reuse your assignments as its stages: the research from assignment two, the prototype from four, the evaluation from five, the test from six.
41  {emphatic} Tip three. | Learn the vocabulary by heart: Norman's six principles, Shneiderman's eight rules, Nielsen's ten heuristics, the four POUR principles, SUS and WCAG. They come up in every presentation and every oral.
42  {warm} To recap: | two credits, one lecture and two lab hours a week; | four units from human factors to AI interfaces; | eight lab assignments and a mini project; | and fifty marks: twenty five continuous, twenty five oral.
