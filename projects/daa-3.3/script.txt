01  {curious} Here are four weights: seven, eleven, thirteen and twenty four kilos. | Can you pick some of them that weigh exactly thirty one?
02  {excited} Two ways: seven plus twenty four, and seven plus eleven plus thirteen. | With forty weights there are over a trillion subsets, and backtracking finds the answers without trying them all.
03  {warm} Welcome to the DAA series. | I'm Abhijeet Karve, and I'll guide you through every algorithm, step by step.
04  {explain} This is Unit Three, video three. | Two more classic backtracking problems: sum of subsets, and the Hamiltonian cycle.
05  {explain} Both appear in exams as draw-the-state-space-tree questions, | so stay till the end for the exam tips.
06  {explain} Sum of subsets. | Given n positive weights and a target m, find every subset whose weights add up to exactly m.
07  {explain} A solution is a tuple of zeros and ones: | x k is one if weight k is chosen, and zero if it is left out.
08  {emphatic} We keep the weights sorted in increasing order: seven, eleven, thirteen, twenty four. | The bounding function depends on that.
09  {explain} Every node remembers three numbers: | s, the sum chosen so far; k, the next weight to decide; and r, the sum of the weights still undecided.
10  {explain} Two bounding checks. | First: s plus everything remaining must reach m, otherwise even taking all of it is not enough.
11  {explain} Second: s plus the next weight must not exceed m, | otherwise we have already overshot.
12  {warm} Let's trace it. | At the root, s is zero, k is one, and r is fifty five, the sum of all four weights.
13  {explain} Weight one is seven. Taking it makes s seven. | That is the left child, x one equals one.
14  {excited} Take eleven as well: s is eighteen. Then take thirteen: s is thirty one. | That equals m, so we have our first answer: seven, eleven, thirteen.
15  {explain} Back up one step. At s eighteen, skipping thirteen leaves only twenty four, | and eighteen plus twenty four is forty two, over m. That node dies.
16  {explain} Back to s seven, and skip eleven. Taking thirteen gives twenty, and twenty plus twenty four is too big. | Skipping thirteen leaves twenty, with nothing left to reach thirty one. Both die.
17  {excited} Skip thirteen as well, so s is still seven, and take twenty four: thirty one. | Second answer: seven and twenty four!
18  {explain} Finally, leave seven out. | Every path on this side overshoots thirty one or cannot reach it, so they all die early.
19  {warm} Done. Sixteen nodes instead of thirty one in the full tree, | and not a single hopeless subset was built.
20  {explain} Here is the algorithm, Sum Of Sub of s, k, r. | It first tries x k equals one, then x k equals zero.
21  {explain} Taking weight k: if the new sum equals m, print the answer; | if the sum plus the next weight is still within m, go deeper.
22  {explain} Leaving weight k: go deeper only if the remaining weights can still reach m, | and the next weight does not overshoot.
23  {explain} There are two to the power n subsets, so the worst case is order two to the n. | The bounding function is what makes it fast in practice.
24  {curious} Now the second problem. | A delivery van must visit five cities and come back home, visiting each city exactly once, using only the roads that exist.
25  {explain} In graph terms, this is a Hamiltonian cycle: | a cycle that passes through every vertex exactly once and returns to the start.
26  {warm} It is named after William Hamilton, | who sold it as a board game puzzle in eighteen fifty seven.
27  {explain} Our tuple is the order of the visit, x one to x n. | We fix x one as vertex one, so the same cycle is not counted n times.
28  {emphatic} The bounding rule for x k has three checks: | it must be adjacent to the previous vertex, it must not be visited already, | and the last vertex must also be adjacent to vertex one, to close the cycle.
29  {explain} Start at one. Vertex two is adjacent, so x two is two. | From two, the smallest unvisited neighbour is three. From three, it is five.
30  {emphatic} The last vertex must be four, the only one left. | Four is adjacent to five, but there is no road from four back to one. Dead end!
31  {explain} Backtrack to x three. After vertex two, the next option is four. | From four we go to five, and from five to three.
32  {excited} Three is adjacent to one, so the cycle closes: | one, two, four, five, three, and back to one. Found!
33  {explain} The algorithm. Next Value of k keeps trying the next vertex for x k | until one passes all three checks.
34  {explain} Hamiltonian of k calls Next Value; | if k equals n it prints the cycle, otherwise it moves on to k plus one.
35  {explain} In the worst case it tries every ordering of the remaining vertices, | order of n factorial. Still far better than listing all orderings first.
36  {explain} Both problems follow the same pattern as the queens: | choose x k, check the bound, go deeper, and come back when stuck.

40  {emphatic} Exam tip one. | For sum of subsets, label every node with s, k and r, and write the two bounding conditions beside the tree.
41  {emphatic} Exam tip two. | Sort the weights first, and say that you did. Without sorting, the second check is not valid.
42  {emphatic} Exam tip three. | For the Hamiltonian cycle, list the three checks of Next Value, and show the dead end before the cycle.
43  {warm} To recap: | sum of subsets tracks s, k and r with two bounding checks, | the Hamiltonian cycle picks the next adjacent unvisited vertex, | and both use the same backtracking control abstraction.
