01  Here are four weights: seven, eleven, thirteen and twenty four kilos. Can you pick some of them that weigh exactly thirty one?
02  Two ways: seven plus twenty four, and seven plus eleven plus thirteen. With forty weights there are over a trillion subsets, and backtracking finds the answers without trying them all.
03  Welcome to the DAA series. I'm Abhijeet Karve, and I'll guide you through every algorithm, step by step.
04  This is Unit Three, video three. Two more classic backtracking problems: sum of subsets, and the Hamiltonian cycle.
05  Both appear in exams as draw-the-state-space-tree questions, so stay till the end for the exam tips.
06  Sum of subsets. Given n positive weights and a target m, find every subset whose weights add up to exactly m.
07  A solution is a tuple of zeros and ones: x k is one if weight k is chosen, and zero if it is left out.
08  We keep the weights sorted in increasing order: seven, eleven, thirteen, twenty four. The bounding function depends on that.
09  Every node remembers three numbers: s, the sum chosen so far; k, the next weight to decide; and r, the sum of the weights still undecided.
10  Two bounding checks. First: s plus everything remaining must reach m, otherwise even taking all of it is not enough.
11  Second: s plus the next weight must not exceed m, otherwise we have already overshot.
12  Let's trace it. At the root, s is zero, k is one, and r is fifty five, the sum of all four weights.
13  Weight one is seven. Taking it makes s seven. That is the left child, x one equals one.
14  Take eleven as well: s is eighteen. Then take thirteen: s is thirty one. That equals m, so we have our first answer: seven, eleven, thirteen.
15  Back up one step. At s eighteen, skipping thirteen leaves only twenty four, and eighteen plus twenty four is forty two, over m. That node dies.
16  Back to s seven, and skip eleven. Taking thirteen gives twenty, and twenty plus twenty four is forty four, too big, so that node dies.
17  Skip thirteen as well, so s is still seven, and take twenty four: thirty one. Second answer: seven and twenty four!
18  Finally, leave seven out. Every path on this side overshoots thirty one or cannot reach it, so they all die early.
19  Done. Sixteen nodes instead of thirty one in the full tree, and not a single hopeless subset was built.
20  Here is the algorithm, Sum Of Sub of s, k, r. It first tries x k equals one, then x k equals zero.
21  Taking weight k: if the new sum equals m, print the answer; if the sum plus the next weight is still within m, go deeper.
22  Leaving weight k: go deeper only if the remaining weights can still reach m, and the next weight does not overshoot.
23  There are two to the power n subsets, so the worst case is order two to the n. The bounding function is what makes it fast in practice.
24  Now the second problem. A delivery van must visit five cities and come back home, visiting each city exactly once, using only the roads that exist.
25  In graph terms, this is a Hamiltonian cycle: a cycle that passes through every vertex exactly once and returns to the start.
26  It is named after William Hamilton, who sold it as a board game puzzle in eighteen fifty seven.
27  Our tuple is the order of the visit, x one to x n. We fix x one as vertex one, so the same cycle is not counted n times.
28  The bounding rule for x k has three checks: it must be adjacent to the previous vertex, it must not be visited already, and the last vertex must also be adjacent to vertex one, to close the cycle.
29  Start at one. Vertex two is adjacent, so x two is two. From two, the smallest unvisited neighbour is three. From three, it is five.
30  The last vertex must be four, the only one left. Four is adjacent to five, but there is no road from four back to one. Dead end!
31  Backtrack to x three. After vertex two, the next option is four. From four we go to five, and from five to three.
32  Three is adjacent to one, so the cycle closes: one, two, four, five, three, and back to one. Found!
33  The algorithm. Next Value of k keeps trying the next vertex for x k until one passes all three checks.
34  Hamiltonian of k calls Next Value; if k equals n it prints the cycle, otherwise it moves on to k plus one.
35  In the worst case it tries every ordering of the remaining vertices, order of n factorial. Still far better than listing all orderings first.
36  Both problems follow the same pattern as the queens: choose x k, check the bound, go deeper, and come back when stuck.
